## Core Mechanics: Push, Pull, Drag, and Climb

Sokoban purists know the classic rule: you can push a block, and pushing is permanent. Lab Rat breaks that rule deliberately, and the puzzle design grows out of the break.

| Action | What It Does | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Push | Shoves a crate one tile forward | The baseline sokoban move you already know |
| Pull | Draws a crate toward you | Lets you undo a bad angle without a full reset |
| Drag sideways | Slides a crate laterally along an axis | Rescues crates that would otherwise be corner-locked |
| Climb | Lets you stand on top of a crate | Reaches ledges and opens new sightlines |
| Zap | Fires a colored charge at one face of a crate | Recolors a face so you can grab it |

That last one is the heart of the system. Each crate has faces that can be altered by color-coded charges from laser guns, and you can only grab the sides that match your own current charge. The palette stays deliberately small — yellow, blue, and green — which keeps the puzzle space readable. A colorblind mode is included, and it matters: without it, the core mechanic would be inaccessible to a meaningful slice of players.

The result is a puzzle game where the difficulty doesn't come from sprawling, open-ended levels. It comes from precision.

### Tight rooms, precise solutions

Rooms in Lab Rat are intentionally compact. Community reports consistently note that you rarely wander into a wildly wrong direction for long — there simply isn't room. Instead, you hunt for the exact sequence of moves that unlocks the chamber.

That design choice has a real benefit for newcomers: failure is cheap. When a room resets in seconds, experimentation stops feeling punishing and starts feeling like the actual game.

## Color Charges, Noir Rooms, and Puzzle Design

Where Lab Rat gets genuinely inventive is in how it varies its own rules without ever abandoning them.

| Room Type | The Twist | Typical Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard test chamber | Teaches push, pull, drag, and climb | Required |
| Color-match rooms | Faces must align with your charge | Required |
| Noir chapters | Visual color is stripped out entirely | Mixed |
| Side rooms | Extra-difficult brain-twisters | Usually optional |
| Genre-pastiche rooms | Mechanics bent toward a specific style | Mixed |

The noir-themed series is the standout example. The game removes color from the visuals, which means you can no longer simply look at a crate and see whether it's blue or yellow. You have to deduce the colors of the crates and lasers around you from context, inference, and the rules you've internalized.

These rooms were mainly crafted by puzzle designer Lucas Le Slo, and the structure shows: they read as a deliberate test of whether you truly understand the system or have just been pattern-matching on color.

### Optional puzzles are where the ceiling lives

One of the smartest structural decisions is that many puzzles can be skipped. You can push forward through the story and return later for the rooms that beat you.

That creates a natural difficulty curve:

- **Reaching an ending** is a medium-difficulty task for most players.
- **Reaching 100% completion** is considerably harder, because the optional rooms are where the designers let loose.

If you want the Rat Lab full game experience without grinding to a halt, treat optional rooms as a post-credits project rather than a gate.

## SARA, the Story, and the Writing Behind Lab Rat

The narrative hook is SARA: a snarky, robotic AI whose obsession with her own metrics has pushed her past the point of professional judgment. Comparisons to GLaDOS from the Portal series are inevitable and fair, but Lab Rat develops its own comedic threads, many of them aimed squarely at data-obsessed practices in video game playtesting.

| Comparison | Shared DNA | Where Lab Rat Diverges |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Portal 2 | Sardonic AI narrator, test-chamber structure | Lab Rat's jokes target playtesting and metrics, not science-facility bureaucracy |
| Kine | Puzzle-and-writing fusion, same studio | Lab Rat adds color-charge mechanics and a much larger mechanical vocabulary |
| Classic sokoban puzzlers | Grid-based block pushing | Movement is expanded well beyond pushing |

Chump Squad founder Gwen Frey served as lead director, and she brought in two fellow BioShock alumni for the project. That pedigree shows in two places: the sharpness of the writing and the density of environmental detail packed into rooms that are, mechanically, quite small.

The writing and the puzzles are frequently intertwined rather than running in parallel. A joke often lands because of what a room just made you do, and a room often feels funny because of how SARA reacts to your solution.
