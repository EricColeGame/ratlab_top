## What Rat Lab Level 4 Really Tests

If you can name a malware family from a hash but freeze the moment someone asks which file it dropped or where it phones home, **Rat Lab level 4** is the stage built for you. The Rat Lab challenge — a threat-intelligence lab published as the "Yellow RAT Lab" on the CyberDefenders platform — hands you almost nothing and expects you to rebuild an intrusion from public reporting. Level 4 is the deep end of that exercise: the point where a single tool stops being enough and every answer has to survive cross-checking.

That distinction matters in real work. Incident response rarely fails at detection; it fails at scope and attribution. Knowing the malware's family name tells you almost nothing about how far it spread, what persistence it left behind, or which firewall rule will actually stop the bleeding. **Rat Lab level 4** is designed to force those harder questions into the open, using the same free, public platforms that working analysts reach for first.

This guide walks through the scenario, the tool stack, the six pivots that carry most of the weight, and the verification habit that separates a guess from a defensible finding.

## How Community Reports Frame the Rat Lab Progression

Walkthroughs of this lab describe the questions slightly differently from one another, but the progression below matches what most community reports cover. Treat it as a mental map rather than an official curriculum.

| Phase | Core question | Go-to source |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | What family is this sample? | Sandbox report search by hash |
| Level 2 | What filename does it impersonate or use? | Multi-engine file details |
| Level 3 | What did it drop, and when was it built? | Portable-executable metadata plus vendor writeups |
| Level 4 | Where does it call home, and can we prove it? | Three independent sources, cross-checked |

The jump from level 3 to **Rat Lab level 4** is qualitative, not just quantitative. Earlier phases are lookup tasks — you find a field, you copy a value. The final phase is an inference task: you gather partial evidence from several places and decide whether they agree.

## The Rat Lab Level 4 Scenario and Starting Artifact

The scenario is deliberately mundane, which is what makes it realistic. During a routine security check at a fictional industrial firm, analysts notice abnormal network traffic from multiple workstations. Employees report that their search queries are being redirected to unfamiliar sites. You are handed a single file and told to figure out the rest.

| Item | Detail | Analyst notes |
|---|---|---|
| Provided file | `hash.txt` | Plain text, shipped inside the challenge archive |
| Value inside | A SHA256 hash of the sample | 64 hexadecimal characters |
| Hash type | SHA256 | Not MD5 or SHA1 — check before pasting |
| Example IOC from this lab | `30E527E45F50D2BA82865C5679A6FA998EE0A1755361AB01673950810D071C85` | Treat as a live indicator; defang before sharing |
| What you are NOT given | The binary itself | All analysis is open-source intelligence |

The absence of the sample is the whole point. You cannot disassemble what you do not have, so the investigation becomes a research exercise: find where other people have already analyzed this exact sample and read their work critically.

## The Rat Lab Level 4 Tool Stack

Four sources carry nearly all the weight in this challenge. Each answers a different kind of question, and none of them is reliable alone.

| Tool | Primary strength | First thing to check |
|---|---|---|
| Hybrid Analysis | Sandbox behavior and a numeric threat score | Report search results for the hash |
| VirusTotal | File metadata, PE headers, detection ratio, domain reputation | The Details tab |
| Vendor threat intelligence (for example, Red Canary writeups) | Dropped files and observed command-and-control infrastructure | Behavior and IOC sections of the report |
| General web search | Independent writeups and IOC feeds that corroborate the above | Family name plus filename |

Two of these platforms are free to use at a basic level: [VirusTotal's official site](https://www.virustotal.com/) and the [CyberDefenders challenge platform](https://cyberdefenders.org/) where the lab itself lives. Vendor reports are usually published as public blog posts. You do not need a commercial subscription to finish this exercise — you need patience and the discipline to check the same fact twice.

## Walkthrough: The Six Rat Lab Level 4 Pivots

The lab's questions map cleanly onto the artifact fields below. Learn the destination, not a memorized answer, because challenge instances and public reports get updated over time.

| # | Objective | Best source | Field to read |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the malware family | Sandbox report list | Verdict and family tag on the top-scoring report |
| 2 | Find the common filename | Multi-engine file details | Original Name under signature info |
| 3 | Determine compilation time | Portable-executable metadata | Compilation timestamp inside the header block |
| 4 | Find when the community first saw it | Submission history | First submission timestamp |
| 5 | Identify the dropped `.dat` file | Vendor threat report | Dropped-file list in the AppData path |
| 6 | Identify the C2 server | Vendor report, behavior tab, sandbox indicators | External systems and memory-pattern domains |

### Pivots 1–3: Build the Profile

Start with the hash. On the sandbox platform, sort or scan the report list for the entry with the highest threat score and open the Windows sandbox report — a score in the low 90s out of 100 signals a sample worth reading closely. Within that report you will find the family name, which becomes your search term for everything that follows.

Then move to the multi-engine file view. The "Original Name" field under signature information is the filename the binary was compiled with, and it is far more useful for hunting than a generic executable name. Directly below it, the header block holds the compilation timestamp — a strong signal for building a timeline of when the tooling was assembled.

### Pivots 4–6: Answer the Hard Questions

Submission history is easy to overlook. The first-submission timestamp tells you how long this sample has been circulating publicly, which is a rough proxy for how long it may have been in an environment before anyone noticed.

The dropped-file question usually cannot be answered from file metadata alone, so switch to vendor threat reports. Look for a `.dat` artifact written into an AppData directory; that path is a common choice because it rarely requires elevated privileges.

For the command-and-control answer, follow the triple-confirmation pattern in the next section. A single mention of a URL is a lead, not a finding.

## Verification: Triple-Confirming the C2

This is the habit that makes **Rat Lab level 4** worth the effort. Any one source can be wrong, stale, or paraphrasing another source. Agreement across independent evidence types is what turns a URL into a blocklist entry.

| Source | Evidence type | Confidence signal |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor report | Observed C2 URL in an incident narrative | Named directly in the writeup |
| Multi-engine behavior tab | Memory-pattern domain or URL | String found inside the sample itself |
| URL reputation lookup | Community and vendor verdicts | Domain flagged by multiple engines |
| Sandbox report | External systems indicator | Connection captured during detonation |

When all four point at the same hostname, you can state the answer without hedging. When only two do, go back and read the raw fields rather than the summaries — summaries compress and occasionally drift.

## Common Mistakes That Cost You Time in Rat Lab Level 4

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Trusting one platform | Summaries omit fields and can lag behind vendor reports | Confirm every answer in a second source |
| Pasting the wrong hash type | SHA1 and MD5 look similar at a glance | Count the characters — 64 means SHA256 |
| Skipping the sandbox report | It is often the fastest route to the family name | Open the highest-scoring report first |
| Looking for dropped files in file metadata | Dropped artifacts appear in behavior data | Check vendor reports and sandbox behavior |
| Writing the C2 as a bare domain | Loses path and protocol context | Record the full URL as observed |
| Ignoring timestamps entirely | Timelines are the fastest route to scope | Log every date you encounter |

A small analyst habit pays off here: keep a running notes file with one line per fact, and cite the source next to each line. When you reach the last question, your notes are already the answer.

## FAQ

**How long does Rat Lab level 4 take to complete?**
Most analysts who are comfortable with sandbox platforms finish the full exercise in one sitting, typically under an hour. **Rat Lab level 4** is not difficult because the tools are obscure — it is slow the first time because you have to remember which tab hides which field. Keep your notes from the first run; the second run takes a fraction of the time.

**Do I need paid accounts or a commercial threat-intelligence subscription?**
No. The investigation can be completed with free tiers of public malware-analysis platforms plus published vendor research. Paid tiers mainly buy faster lookups and higher API limits, neither of which changes the outcome of the exercise.

**Can I skip straight to the command-and-control question?**
You can try, but you will make it harder on yourself. **Rat Lab level 4** builds on the earlier answers: the family name narrows your vendor-report search, and the filename helps confirm you are reading a report about the right sample rather than a similar variant.

**What should I do with the indicators once I have them?**
Record each one with its source and the date you observed it, then use the missing pieces — the C2 URL for blocking, the filename and dropped `.dat` for host-based hunting, and the compilation timestamp for timeline reconstruction. That combination is what turns a lab answer into a real defensive action.

## Wrapping Up

The Rat Lab challenge is less about memorizing where a field lives and more about learning to distrust a single source. Work the pivots in order, verify the command-and-control answer across at least three independent evidence types, and keep a sourced note for every fact you collect. Do that, and **Rat Lab level 4** stops being the stage that stalls you and becomes the one that shows you how real threat-intelligence work actually gets done.
