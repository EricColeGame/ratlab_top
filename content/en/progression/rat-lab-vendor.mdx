## What the Rat Lab Is and Why It Matters

Getting into the Rat Lab has very little to do with grades and almost everything to do with proving you can finish something by yourself. That single truth explains why so many students searching for a **Rat Lab vendor** — a place to buy kits, parts, and tools — never make it past the first email. The **Rat Lab vendor** question is really a project question in disguise: what will you build, and what do you need to build it?

The Rat Lab, often written RATLab, is a student research collective at Worcester Polytechnic Institute that brings together two groups: the Interactive Music Systems Lab and the Electric Guitar Innovation Lab. Its members, known as Lab RATs, design instruments, assemble electronics, print custom parts, and run experiments in music technology. It is a working research lab with a defined membership track — not a club, not a shop, and not a casual hangout.

For the primary source, the [official Rat Lab member information page](https://theratlab.org/RAT_ARA.html) is worth reading before you email anyone.

Why does this matter? Because the payoff is unusual for an undergraduate. Members get round-the-clock access to a room full of equipment, instruments, and people who share their obsessions. That kind of access is the real prize, and it is earned through a process rather than granted on request.

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| What is the Rat Lab? | A collective of two WPI research labs focused on interactive music systems and electric guitar innovation |
| What are members called? | Lab RATs |
| How do you join? | Complete a voluntary individual project with the PI, then get invited |
| How long is the commitment? | A two-term position once you are invited |
| Signature perk | Round-the-clock access to lab equipment, instruments, and supplies |
| What is the vibe? | Team-oriented and social — shared meals, coffee chats, and collaborative builds |

## The Rat Lab Progression Path, Stage by Stage

Progression is the heart of the Rat Lab experience. Nobody walks in as a full member on day one. The path begins with a voluntary, self-directed project completed with the principal investigator — sometimes called a RAT-of-Passage project. It is a mutual test. You find out whether you enjoy the work, and the PI finds out whether you actually finish things without being chased.

Typical first projects are deliberately modest: learning to model and 3D print a small part, or soldering and assembling an electronics device from a kit. The point is not ambition. The point is evidence of follow-through.

When the trial project goes well and the working relationship clicks, the student is invited to commit to a two-term position as a Lab RAT. Only at that stage do the bigger benefits unlock — the 24/7 access, the ability to borrow instruments and equipment, and the freedom to run personal projects alongside incoming research work.

| Stage | What you do | Signal you are ready to move on |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Outreach | Email the PI with a specific interest and a realistic idea | The PI replies and proposes a trial project |
| 2. Trial project | Complete a small independent build or modification | You finish it and can explain your decisions |
| 3. Invitation | Confirm fit with the PI and accept the two-term commitment | You are formally a Lab RAT |
| 4. Active member | Balance lab research with at least one personal project | Your projects keep shipping and get documented |
| 5. Senior track | Take on leadership, mentoring, and publication credit | You are trusted with larger or shared builds |

The lab's own pages also list ARAs alongside Lab RATs, which points to a longer-term track for experienced members. Treat that as something to ask the PI about directly rather than something to assume you can apply for.

## What "Rat Lab Vendor" Really Means in Practice

Here is the honest answer: the Rat Lab itself is not a vendor, and it does not publish an approved supplier list. When people search for a **Rat Lab vendor**, they usually fall into one of two groups — students who need parts for a trial project, and hobbyists who want the same kinds of tools and instruments the lab uses. Both are reasonable, and both get the same advice.

Inside the lab, shared stock and the PI's personal collection come first. Borrowing before buying is the norm, and that habit keeps student costs low. Outside the lab, ordinary component and tool suppliers do the job perfectly well. What matters is that you buy the right category of thing, not that you buy it from any particular brand.

| Vendor category | What you are sourcing | Why it matters for lab work |
|---|---|---|
| Electronics component distributors | Jacks, pots, switches, wire, connectors | Prototypes change fast, so buy spares of cheap parts |
| Microcontroller and audio board suppliers | Dev boards, sensors, audio modules | The core of most interactive music builds |
| 3D printing filament and resin sellers | PLA, PETG, resin, nozzles | Custom mounts, brackets, and modified instrument parts |
| Soldering and bench tool vendors | Irons, tips, solder, flux, multimeters | Assembly quality decides whether a project works |
| Luthier and guitar parts suppliers | Tuners, pickups, bridges, fret wire | Essential for electric guitar innovation work |
| Kit retailers | Learn-to-solder kits and DIY electronics kits | The classic, low-risk first trial project |

A few practical rules save both money and goodwill. Ask the PI what the lab already has before ordering anything. Buy one spare of any part under a few dollars, because a single fried component can stall a build for a week. And check whether a supplier offers an educational discount — many do, and the savings add up quickly on a student budget. Community reports from students who have run independent builds suggest that students who over-order parts they never use regret it far more often than students who under-order and improvise.

## Project Ideas, Gear, and the Vendors Behind Them

Choosing the right first project is the single highest-leverage decision you will make. Aim for something finishable in two to four weeks with one clear deliverable. A small, polished, documented build beats an ambitious half-finished one every time, because the PI is evaluating your process as much as your result.

| Project idea | Skill you demonstrate | Typical parts involved |
|---|---|---|
| 3D-printed replacement part | CAD modeling and print tolerances | Filament, calipers, sandpaper |
| Assemble a solder kit | Soldering, wiring, debugging | Kit, iron, flux, solder |
| Modify a guitar pedal | Circuit reading, audio troubleshooting | Enclosure, pots, jacks, knobs |
| Build a small MIDI controller | Embedded coding, input handling | Microcontroller, buttons, encoders |
| Sensor-driven instrument | Signal processing, iteration | Sensors, audio board, wiring |

Notice how each idea maps cleanly onto a vendor category from the table above. That is not a coincidence — the supplies you need tell you how hard a project really is. If a build requires six different supplier categories, it is probably too big for a trial project.

Documentation matters too. Take photos at each stage, keep your parts list, and write a short summary of what broke and how you fixed it. That record is what turns a one-off build into something the lab can build on, and it is often what earns the invitation to join.

## Access, Perks, and How to Stand Out

Once you are in, the role comes with real perks — and each one is paired with an expectation. The culture is explicitly team-oriented: members eat together, trade ideas over coffee and snacks, and help each other's builds succeed. Credit flows to the whole group through publications and project write-ups, so individual hoarding of work runs against the grain.

| Perk | Paired responsibility |
|---|---|
| 24/7 access to the lab | Keep shared benches and tools usable for everyone |
| Borrowing instruments and equipment | Return items in the same condition you found them |
| Freedom to run personal projects | Keep at least one project in active development |
| Shared credit on publications | Contribute reliably to team projects |
| Access to a knowledgeable peer network | Help newer members learn the basics |

A handful of habits separate students who thrive from students who fade out:

- **Start smaller than feels impressive.** A finished small build opens doors; an unfinished grand one closes them.
- **Communicate before you disappear.** A quick update during a slow week beats silence followed by an apology.
- **Ask about materials early.** Confirm what you can borrow before you spend money with any Rat Lab vendor.
- **Show up socially.** Shared meals and informal conversations are where collaborations and opportunities actually begin.
- **Track your own progress.** Keep a simple log of what you built, learned, and would do differently.
- **Look beyond the term.** Ask about the ARA track and what experienced members do differently.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there an official Rat Lab vendor I should buy from?**
No. The lab is a research collective, not a storefront, and it does not publish a supplier list. Most people looking for a Rat Lab vendor are really looking for the right category of parts — electronics components, 3D printing supplies, bench tools, or instrument hardware. Buy from a reputable supplier in that category, and check with the lab about shared stock first.

**How long does it take to go from applicant to Lab RAT?**
There is no fixed timeline, because it depends on how long your trial project takes and how well the working relationship develops. A focused project completed in a few weeks gives the PI enough information to make a call. The invitation itself, once extended, comes with a two-term commitment.

**Do I need to be a WPI student to get involved?**
The Rat Lab is based at Worcester Polytechnic Institute and its membership is tied to that community. If you are outside it, the most honest next step is to contact the lab directly and ask what, if anything, is possible.

**What should I buy before my first project?**
Very little. Ask what the lab already has, then purchase only the specific parts your build requires — plus a spare or two of anything cheap and easily destroyed. Resist the urge to stockpile. Starting a conversation with the PI before placing an order is almost always the better first move.
